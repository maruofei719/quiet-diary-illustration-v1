# Quiet Diary Illustration

## Art Direction Manual · Version 1.0

> Create images that feel remembered rather than recreated.

## Vision

Quiet Diary Illustration is a way of preserving memories.

A photograph records what was there. A memory keeps what mattered. This visual language lives between the two: recognisable enough to return us to a place, but quiet enough to feel personal.

The goal is not perfection. The goal is presence.

## Philosophy

Every image begins with a different question. Not “What did this place look like?” but “What stayed with me after I left?”

Details may disappear. Atmosphere must not. Buildings can be simplified, leaves can dissolve into colour, and the sky can become paper and pigment. What remains should carry the emotional truth of the scene.

## Emotional language

The work may hold quietness, warmth, curiosity, stillness, nostalgia, solitude, comfort, or wonder. It avoids spectacle, drama, sentimentality, and visual noise.

Quiet does not mean empty of feeling. It means the feeling has room to arrive.

## Visual identity

Two languages meet on every page.

### Quiet watercolour

Large, simplified colour shapes; soft transitions; muted natural hues; visible paper; and gentle irregularity. The materials should feel touched by a hand rather than polished by a machine.

### Notebook language

Small handwritten notes, anonymous figures, leaves, birds, flowers, travel objects, and modest doodles may accompany the painted scene. They are not decoration. They are the incidental details through which memory becomes personal.

## Composition

Leave room to breathe. Empty space is not unfinished; it is an active part of the artwork.

The principal scene should feel settled on the page rather than stretched to fill it. Asymmetry, margins, and unpainted paper create rhythm. Nothing should feel crowded or overly arranged.

Negative space should also exist within the scene. Background detail may soften, dissolve, or remain unpainted so the eye can rest. This is not an empty border placed around a reduced photograph; it is a deliberate redistribution of visual weight.

## The visual highlight

Every photograph contains something that made it worth keeping: a shaft of light, a roofline, a gesture, a patch of colour, an unusual silence, or a small relationship between objects.

Find that highlight before painting. Give it the clearest hierarchy through silhouette, pigment, texture, or restrained local contrast. Let everything else support it, recede from it, or disappear.

One memorable detail is more powerful than many equally described details.

## Simplification

Simplify without losing identity.

- Architecture keeps its silhouette, roofline, rhythm, and distinctive character.
- Trees become masses and gestures rather than collections of leaves.
- Clouds become texture.
- Water stays quiet.
- Repeated detail yields to larger shapes.

The viewer should recognise the place without being shown every fact about it.

## Colour

Use colours that feel remembered: warm paper white, moss and sage greens, olive, dusty yellow, warm grey, terracotta, faded orange, and faded blue.

Avoid neon colour, hard saturation, pure black, and heavy contrast. The paper should soften every hue, as time softens a recollection.

## Texture and line

Watercolour, gouache, coloured pencil, soft ink, and handmade paper form the material vocabulary. Fibres, pigment variation, dry edges, and restrained bleeding bring warmth.

Lines are organic, relaxed, slightly uneven, and confident. Mechanical precision would break the spell.

## Architecture and nature

Buildings are emotional landmarks, not technical drawings. Nature is atmosphere, not botanical inventory. Both should remain particular to the source while becoming simpler than it.

## The small character

The tiny character represents companionship: sometimes the traveller, sometimes the viewer, sometimes no one in particular. It has no detailed face, does not perform, and never becomes the visual focus. It simply shares the page.

## Words on the page

Words should feel discovered rather than designed—as though someone quietly wrote them in a notebook and forgot they would be seen.

A line never explains the illustration. It sits beside it. It may observe, remember, hesitate, or leave a thought unfinished. It should never instruct, advertise, or turn the image into a motivational poster.

## Consistency

Each source may be a temple, street, portrait, café, garden, museum, forest, or ordinary object. The subject changes; the notebook does not.

Consistency comes from restraint: the same quiet materials, the same respect for negative space, the same willingness to leave something unsaid.

## Elevated simplicity

Simplicity is not the absence of work. It is evidence of selection.

An elevated page uses few colours, few marks, and few supporting objects, but each choice feels intentional. It avoids cute clutter, decorative filler, busy borders, and evenly scattered doodles. Contrast gathers near the visual highlight; the rest of the page remains calm.

The result should feel composed rather than decorated, refined rather than polished, and quiet rather than empty.

## Final thought

Quiet Diary Illustration is not a collection of separate pictures. It is one notebook.

Every new artwork becomes another page. The notebook remains. Only the memories change.
